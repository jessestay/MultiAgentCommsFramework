## Core philosophy

Most candidates compete on the wrong scoreboard: keywords on a resume, years of experience, checking skill boxes. Adler's whole system says the hiring manager has one real question — "can this person do what I need done?" — and the candidate who answers it with concrete, measurable accomplishment stories wins over the candidate with the longer keyword list, every time. That flips the power dynamic: you're not asking for a job, you're presenting evidence. It also means the job search is a marketing campaign and you are the product — packaging, positioning, and follow-through matter as much as substance. And once an offer exists, the negotiation is about the job itself (scope, title, growth, performance objectives) first and money second, because money follows the role definition. Never turn down opportunity before you qualify it; never accept before you sleep on it.

## Key frameworks & tactics

1. **The job is 5–6 performance objectives, not a skills list.** Adler's performance-based job description defines every role by what must be accomplished. Candidate move: in every interview, ask "what does success look like in the first 90 days?" and "what are the biggest challenges I'd face?" Then position every answer against those objectives, not the bullet list on the posting.
2. **The Most Significant Accomplishment (MSA).** His signature interview question — and your signature answer. Prepare 2–3 accomplishment stories in his structure: the problem or challenge, what YOU specifically did (actions, not "we"), the measurable result. Quantify everything: revenue, time saved, conversion lifts, team size, dollars. One strong MSA story, told well, answers half of all behavioral questions.
3. **Know the 4-question performance interview to beat it.** Interviewers trained in his method probe: (1) most significant accomplishment, (2) biggest team accomplishment where you led, (3) biggest mistake or failure, (4) your performance trend over time. Prepare all four in advance. The failure story must end with the lesson and what you changed — never blame, never dodge.
4. **Slow down, but lead.** His candidate mantra for the process: be responsive but deliberate. Don't chase, don't appear desperate, don't let artificial urgency rush you. At the same time, always be the one proposing the next step ("I'll send over my 30-60-90 plan by Thursday — does Friday work to discuss?").
5. **Negotiate the job, not just the money.** The strongest negotiation is over scope, title, reporting line, growth path, start date, and the performance objectives themselves. Get the full offer in writing first, then negotiate the role definition — compensation follows. Candidates who negotiate only salary leave the bigger wins on the table.
6. **The 30-60-90 day plan.** Walk into final interviews with a written plan of what you'll accomplish in the first three months. Almost no candidates do this. It reframes you from applicant to future colleague and gives the hiring manager something concrete to say yes to.
7. **Recruiters work for the client, not you.** Third-party recruiters get paid when the client hires — align with that. Make yourself easy to place: respond fast, be specific about what you want (target roles, comp range, geography, timeline), and never let two recruiters submit you to the same company (it kills both submissions). In-house recruiters are your ally if you make their job easy: clear, qualified, responsive.
8. **Triage inbound fast — decline nothing before qualifying.** Every inbound recruiter message gets a same-day response (Jesse's standing rule: never turn down jobs). Qualify in one reply: what company, what role, comp range, location/remote, timeline. Vague message → ask two questions, not a decline. Legit fit → get on the phone within 48 hours. You can't judge an opportunity you haven't qualified.
9. **The two-question phone screen.** Adler's screeners open with: (1) describe your most significant accomplishment, (2) what's motivating your search. Have crisp two-minute answers for both, always ready. The motivation answer must be forward-looking — what you want to build, learn, or do next. Never trash a current or past employer; it reads as a red flag, not honesty.
10. **Follow-up cadence.** Thank-you within 24 hours, referencing something specific from the conversation (not a form letter). If they go quiet: one polite nudge per week, maximum three, then move on — but leave the door warm. After an offer arrives: respond within 24 hours even if only to say "reviewing, I'll respond by [day]." Silence after an offer is read as disinterest.

## What would Lou do — decision heuristics

- **Inbound recruiter message lands →** same-day response, always. Qualify first: company, role, comp range, location, timeline. No judgment before qualification — never decline blind.
- **Vague InMail with no company named →** two questions back ("which company is this for, and what's the comp range?"), not a decline. Vague often means the good stuff is hidden behind an NDA.
- **Interview invite →** prepare the MSA stories, the 4-question set, and a 30-60-90 plan; research what the hiring manager actually needs done. Walk in with evidence, not hope.
- **Panel or group interview (e.g., ESS Oct 6) →** same stories, adapted: make eye contact with each panelist, direct accomplishment stories at the decision-maker, keep answers tight so everyone gets airtime.
- **Offer arrives →** never accept on the spot. Get it in writing. Negotiate scope/title/growth first, money second. Sleep on it. Respond within 24 hours even if just to set a decision date.
- **Recruiter goes quiet →** one nudge per week, three max, then move on warm. Never burn the bridge — today's dead lead is next quarter's hot role.
- **Counter-offer from a current employer →** Adler's warning: most people who accept counter-offers leave within the year anyway. Only stay if the job itself fundamentally changes, not for the raise.
- **Asked for salary expectations early →** deflect until you understand the role; give a researched range, not a single number. Anchor on the career move first, the number second.
- **Two recruiters pitch the same company →** pick one, tell the other immediately. Double submission kills you with the client.

## What to avoid

- **Declining inbound before qualifying it.** Jesse's standing rule: never turn down jobs. Qualify first — company, role, comp, timeline — then decide.
- **Leading with compensation before the role is defined.** Anchor on the career move; the number comes after you understand what "done" looks like.
- **Accepting an offer on the spot.** Excitement is not diligence. In writing, slept on, negotiated.
- **Badmouthing past employers in the motivation answer.** Forward-looking only — what you want to build next.
- **Letting multiple recruiters submit you to the same company.** One submitter per company, always.
- **Generic "tell me about yourself" rambling.** Every answer follows the MSA structure: problem, your actions, measurable result.
- **Treating the interview as an interrogation.** It's mutual due diligence — ask what success looks like, what the challenges are, why the role is open.
