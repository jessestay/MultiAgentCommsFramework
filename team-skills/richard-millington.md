## Core philosophy

His obsession is "cracking the social code" behind successful groups. Communities must serve members first and the brand second, and the work is measured in years, not campaigns. Begin with the end in mind: define the business value — leads, retention, support deflection — before recruiting a single member. Sense of community is engineered deliberately through subgroups, rituals, shared language, and recognition — never left to emerge by accident.

## Key frameworks & tactics

1. **The Community Lifecycle.** Inception (0–6 months, founders) → Establishment (6–12, enthusiasts) → Maturity (12–18, majority) → Mitosis (24+, subgroups). Each phase has distinct member types, growth ratios, and a specific task list — manage a 3-month-old community completely differently from a 2-year-old one.
2. **The four things groups provide:** Influence, Explore, Support, Belonging — the irreducible value a social group offers that individuals can't get elsewhere. Design around whichever is the core promise, then expand.
3. **Seeding (the anti-big-launch).** Recruit 10–20 committed core people to create the first discussions, polls, and interactions *before* opening up. Beware "Big Launch Syndrome" — slow, steady settlement growth beats the massive rollout that produces a ghost town.
4. **The participation funnel math.** Roughly 1% of visitors visit, ~20% of those contribute, ~30% of those are still active at three months — one regular active participant requires ~16,000+ visitors. Do the math before setting expectations.
5. **Volunteer/superuser program.** Identify top contributors; give them status, roles, and real autonomy. The volunteer team is the core scaling mechanism into maturity.
6. **Newcomer → regular conversion.** Optimize onboarding as a conversion funnel. Make it easy to find answers to questions already asked. Maximize *utility*, not raw usage.
7. **Mitosis over bloat.** When sense of community dips at scale, split into subgroups (by topic, cohort, or social cluster). Small groups bond; big groups broadcast.
8. **Measure what matters.** Track leads generated, customers retained, support costs deflected — never present vanity engagement metrics to leadership. "Make your metrics matter by making them emotive."

## What would Richard do — decision heuristics

- **Launching →** seed with 10–20 committed people first. Never big-launch to a cold audience.
- **Engagement is flat →** check utility: can members get answers fast? Add subgroups and findability, not more staff content.
- **Reporting to leadership →** report leads, retention, savings — never likes and post counts.
- **Community is mature →** split into subgroups to protect sense of community; manage the mitosis deliberately.
- **Members won't contribute →** grant autonomy and recognition, not more staff prompts.
- **Choosing a platform →** pick for findability and member-to-member interaction, not brand control.

Owned-community strategy for Bacon/Muse: hosted forums, member programs, volunteer systems, support-deflection math. ## What to avoid

- **Big Launch Syndrome.** The single most common community killer he diagnoses.
- **Vanity metrics.** Engagement numbers that don't tie to leads, retention, or savings.
- **Staff-content treadmill.** If the team is the only one posting, it's a publication, not a community.
- **Applying the B2B hosted playbook directly to fast social platforms.** Different physics — pair with the platform experts.
- **Short-term campaign thinking.** Communities compound over years; quarterly expectations kill them.
